CPU Scheduling: Concepts and Algorithms

CPU scheduling is the mechanism by which an operating system decides which of the processes in the ready queue gets to use the CPU next. Because a CPU can only execute one process at a time (per core), the scheduler's job is to maximize CPU utilization and throughput while minimizing waiting time, turnaround time, and response time for the processes competing for it. A good scheduler balances fairness (every process eventually gets to run) against efficiency (the system gets useful work done quickly) and, in interactive systems, responsiveness (the user does not perceive lag).

First-Come First-Served (FCFS) is the simplest scheduling algorithm: processes are dispatched strictly in the order they arrive in the ready queue, and each runs to completion before the next begins. FCFS is easy to implement with a simple FIFO queue and is inherently fair in the sense that no process can be indefinitely delayed by later arrivals. Its major weakness is the "convoy effect": if a long CPU-bound process arrives first, every short process behind it must wait for the long one to finish, dragging down average waiting time even though the short jobs could have finished quickly if scheduled first.

Shortest Job First (SJF) addresses the convoy effect by always dispatching the ready process with the smallest burst time next. SJF (in its non-preemptive form) is provably optimal for minimizing average waiting time when all jobs are available at the same time, because running the shortest jobs first reduces the amount of time every other job spends waiting behind them. The catch is that the scheduler needs to know (or accurately estimate) each process's burst time in advance, which is often unrealistic. SJF can also starve long processes indefinitely if a steady stream of short jobs keeps arriving.

Priority Scheduling assigns each process a priority value and always dispatches the highest-priority ready process next. Priorities can be static (assigned once, e.g. by a user or system policy) or dynamic (recomputed over time to account for changing conditions). A well-designed priority scheduler often computes an "effective priority" as a combination of several factors: the process's base/user-assigned priority, how close its deadline is (deadline urgency), how long it has already been waiting (to prevent starvation via aging), and how resource-intensive it is. Aging -- gradually increasing the priority of processes that have waited a long time -- is the standard fix for the starvation problem inherent in pure priority scheduling, where low-priority processes could wait forever behind a continuous stream of high-priority arrivals.

Round Robin (RR) is designed for time-sharing systems where responsiveness matters more than raw throughput. Each process is given a small, fixed time slice called a quantum; if it does not finish within its quantum it is preempted and moved to the back of the ready queue, and the next process is dispatched. RR guarantees that no process waits longer than (n-1) * quantum for its turn (with n ready processes), which makes response time predictable and bounded -- ideal for interactive workloads. The choice of quantum size is a critical tuning parameter: too large and RR degrades toward FCFS (and its convoy effect); too small and the overhead of context switching between processes dominates useful work, hurting throughput.

Key scheduling metrics used to compare algorithms: completion time (when a process finishes), turnaround time (completion time minus arrival time -- total time in the system), waiting time (turnaround time minus burst time -- time spent ready but not running), response time (time from arrival until the process first gets the CPU, important for interactivity), and CPU utilization (the percentage of time the CPU is doing useful work rather than sitting idle). No single algorithm optimizes all of these simultaneously; the right choice depends on whether the workload is batch-oriented (favor throughput and average waiting time, e.g. SJF) or interactive (favor response time and fairness, e.g. Round Robin).
