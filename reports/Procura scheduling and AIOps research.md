# Procura's textbook core has real production heirs

Procura's premise — a to-do list run through FCFS, SJF, Priority, and Round Robin, with an AI layer that explains decisions and heals simulated failures — rests on genuinely sound ground, but each pillar has a production-grade successor that the research surfaced concretely. **Linux stopped using the textbook four decades ago**: CFS ran from kernel 2.6.23 (2007) until EEVDF replaced it in kernel 6.6 (2023/2024), and both are precisely formalizable in code ([Linux kernel docs: sched-eevdf](https://docs.kernel.org/scheduler/sched-eevdf.html)). **Kubernetes, Slurm, and Celery are living, documented analogues of exactly what Procura simulates** — pluggable scheduling frameworks, weighted multifactor priority with backfilling, and priority-tiered task routing, respectively. **The AIOps "detect → diagnose → remediate" loop Procura proposes is not a toy metaphor but the literal architecture of Kubernetes controllers, Datadog Watchdog, Dynatrace Davis AI, and a fast-growing 2023–2025 arXiv literature on LLM/RAG-based cloud root-cause analysis**, some evaluated against 100,000+ production incidents. **Naive fixed-threshold anomaly detection is demonstrably inferior to EWMA, Z-score/modified-Z-score, and CUSUM**, all of which have exact NIST-specified formulas and are the same math underlying Prometheus, Grafana, and CloudWatch anomaly bands. **Banker's Algorithm is academically celebrated but essentially unimplemented in production** — real systems use timeout, lock ordering, or timestamp-ordering (wound-wait) instead, for reasons the research pins down precisely. And **rigor in evaluation is a solved, citable problem**: the Parallel Workloads Archive, Google cluster traces, and Jain's Fairness Index give Procura an exact, standard methodology to benchmark its five algorithms against, rather than inventing ad hoc metrics. Together, this body of research converts Procura from "a fun OS-concepts demo" into a project that can honestly claim grounding in production scheduler design, SRE/AIOps architecture, applied statistics, and distributed-systems concurrency control — provided the upgrades below are actually implemented, not just name-dropped.

## CFS and EEVDF give Procura a defensible fifth and sixth algorithm

The clearest, most concrete upgrade path is replacing "textbook Round Robin" with the two algorithms that actually run on billions of Linux machines. **CFS keeps every runnable task in a red-black tree keyed by virtual runtime (vruntime), always dispatching the leftmost (smallest-vruntime) node**, with vruntime advancing as `delta_exec * NICE_0_LOAD / weight` — so a higher-weight (lower-nice) task's vruntime grows more slowly per unit of real CPU time, which is precisely why it gets a larger effective share ([Linux CFS doc](https://gitea.hillion.co.uk/JakeHillion/scx-upstream/raw/branch/experimental-scheds/ai_docs/LINUX_CFS.md)). The nice-to-weight mapping has a clean closed form, `Weight ≈ 1024 / 1.25^nice` ([CS162 Berkeley lecture 13](https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs162/fa23/static/lectures/13.pdf)), though the actual kernel uses a hand-tuned lookup table (`sched_prio_to_weight[40]`) that only approximates this ratio — nice 0 maps to weight 1024, nice −20 to 88761, nice +19 to 15 ([LKML thread](https://lkml.iu.edu/1910.0/07223.html)). A task's slice within a scheduling period is proportional to its weight share, `slice_i = (weight_i / Σweight_j) * sched_latency_ns`, with `sched_latency_ns` ≈ 24ms and a 3ms floor (`sched_min_granularity_ns`) to bound context-switch overhead. This is a five-field implementation: a sorted structure keyed by vruntime, a weight derived from priority, a per-tick vruntime update, always dispatching the minimum, and an optional slice floor/ceiling.

**EEVDF is the stronger and more current citation**, since it is what Linux actually runs today, not CFS. Each task carries a "lag" relative to the ideal fair-share schedule — positive lag means it's owed CPU time and is "eligible" to run; only eligible tasks are considered — and among eligible tasks the scheduler always picks the one with the earliest virtual deadline, computed as eligible time plus allocated slice ([Linux kernel docs: sched-eevdf](https://docs.kernel.org/scheduler/sched-eevdf.html); [LWN.net Article 925371](https://lwn.net/Articles/925371)). Tasks can even request a specific desired time slice via `sched_setattr()`, with shorter requested slices earning earlier deadlines and hence priority — a mechanism directly portable to Procura as "a task can declare its own desired quantum." Framing this to the user matters: Procura can legitimately say "CFS is already legacy; we implement its production successor," which is a stronger and more current claim than most student OS projects make. Layered underneath both, **MLFQ remains the best-documented adaptive, behavior-inferring algorithm** — OSTEP's five canonical rules (new jobs enter at top priority; full-allotment consumption triggers demotion; periodic global boost to the top queue every period S prevents starvation) give Procura a fully specified, simulate-able third tier distinct from CFS/EEVDF's continuous-fairness model ([OSTEP Ch. 8 MLFQ](https://fog.misty.com/perry/os/Chapters/08-cpu-sched-mlfq/all-notes.html)). Crucially, the "better accounting" rule — tracking cumulative time used at a level rather than resetting on voluntary yield — exists specifically to close a gaming exploit (a task yielding just before its slice ends to stay at high priority indefinitely), which is a genuinely interesting edge case Procura could visualize as a "gamed scheduler" demo.

Fairness and starvation are where Procura can make its comparison rigorous rather than impressionistic. **FCFS, SJF/SRTF, and static Priority have no starvation bound at all** — SJF can starve a long job indefinitely under continuous short-job arrival, and non-preemptive policies additionally suffer the convoy effect, where one long job blocks a line of short ones ([CS162 lecture 10](https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs162/fa23/static/lectures/10.pdf); [CS162 lecture 11](https://inst.eecs.berkeley.edu/~cs162/fa23/static/lectures/11.pdf)). Round Robin gives a deterministic worst-case wait of `(n−1)·q` between a job's turns for a fixed job set; MLFQ bounds the wait by the boost interval S; CFS/EEVDF bound it structurally because divergence from the ideal fair share cannot grow unboundedly before the scheduler self-corrects. **Jain's Fairness Index** — `J(x) = (Σxᵢ)² / (n·Σxᵢ²)`, ranging from 1/n (maximally unfair) to 1 (perfectly equal), originating from a 1984 Jain/Chiu/Hawe DEC technical report ([arXiv cs/9809099](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/9809099); [WUSTL hosted 1984 report](https://classes.engineering.wustl.edu/~jain/papers/fairness.htm)) — is the exact, unambiguous, implementable metric to put a number on this comparison: track each simulated task's cumulative CPU time at the end of a run, plug the vector into the formula, and report a fairness score per algorithm. This single addition converts "Procura shows five scheduling animations" into "Procura computes and visualizes which algorithm is more fair, quantitatively."

## Kubernetes, Slurm, and Celery are Procura's real-world mirror, not just inspiration

The research found that Procura's core metaphor — treating to-dos like OS processes — already exists at industrial scale under different names, and borrowing their exact mechanics gives Procura concrete, citable design upgrades rather than invented ones. **Kubernetes' kube-scheduler is a "scheduling framework" of pluggable extension points** (PreFilter, Filter, Score, Reserve, Permit, Bind, and others) where filtering candidate nodes and scoring them are separate, swappable stages ([Kubernetes v1.33 Scheduling Framework docs](https://v1-33.docs.kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/scheduling-eviction/scheduling-framework/)). Priority (`PriorityClass`, an integer) and fairness/placement (bin-packing via `NodeResourcesFit`'s `MostAllocated` strategy, versus the default spreading via `LeastAllocated`) are explicitly treated as **two separate axes** — a structure Procura should mirror by decoupling "task priority" (which drives preemption order) from "placement/load-balancing policy" (how tasks are distributed across simulated worker capacity), rather than conflating them into one slider. Preemption itself is implemented as a plugin (`DefaultPreemption`) that evicts lower-priority pods only when a pending pod cannot otherwise be scheduled, with a default 30-second grace period for the evicted workload ([Kubernetes scheduler config reference](https://v1-33.docs.kubernetes.io/docs/reference/scheduling/config); [scheduler deep-dive](https://sia.hackernoon.com/inside-kubernetes-scheduling-how-your-pods-fight-for-a-place-to-exist)) — a clean, literal analogue for "a high-priority task bumps a lower-priority one, which gets a grace period before being fully stopped."

**Slurm's fair-share priority system is the single strongest real-world precedent for a multi-factor priority formula.** Slurm computes one composite priority per pending job as a weighted sum of several factors normalized to [0,1] — age, fair-share, job size, QOS, partition — where the fair-share factor specifically lowers priority for users/accounts that have consumed more than their allocated historical share, with that historical usage decaying over time via a configurable half-life (`PriorityDecayHalfLife`) ([Bayreuth HPC scheduling docs](https://www.hpc.uni-bayreuth.de/advanced/schedandprio/); [Fastone fair-share guide](https://docs.fastonetech.com/en/fsched/admin_guide/公平调度策略Fairshare)). This is a directly portable feature: Procura's priority score could be `w1*urgency + w2*fair_share_decay + w3*age`, where fair_share_decay penalizes a project/list that has already consumed a disproportionate share of "simulated CPU time," exactly mirroring Slurm's anti-monopoly mechanism — a feature few to-do apps have and one clearly traceable to a named, real scheduling system rather than invented from scratch. Slurm also separately runs a **backfill scheduler**: a lower-priority job may start early only if doing so provably will not delay any higher-priority job's projected start time, which requires reasonably accurate time estimates to work ([Slurm official Scheduling Configuration Guide](https://slurm.schedmd.com/sched_config.html)). This "fill idle capacity opportunistically without ever delaying the important thing" rule is exactly the behavior a sophisticated to-do scheduler wants and gives Procura a named, citable algorithm (not an ad-hoc heuristic) for "squeeze in small tasks."

**Celery's design choice is itself an instructive data point: fine-grained numeric priority is unreliable in practice**, so Celery and its community favor routing tasks to dedicated queues/workers with tuned concurrency over per-message priority, and even where priority exists (RabbitMQ's `x-max-priority`), Redis emulates it only coarsely, consolidating into just 4 discrete priority levels by default ([Celery official routing docs](https://docs.celeryq.dev/en/main/userguide/routing.html); [Reintech on Celery task prioritization](https://reintech.io/blog/task-prioritization-in-celery)). This is a useful, slightly contrarian citation for Procura: rather than presenting a continuous 1–100 priority slider as self-evidently "more sophisticated," Procura can note that real distributed systems often deliberately coarsen priority into a handful of discrete tiers because fine-grained priority becomes unreliable and hard to reason about — a defensible design rationale, not just a limitation. On the "is this an established academic problem" question, the literature is thinner than expected: no paper applies MLFQj/CFS/aging directly to personal to-do lists, but a real and citable adjacent literature exists — bug-triage papers using FIFO/SPT/WSPT policies explicitly borrowed from CPU scheduling theory ([Research Square preprint](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-9146893/v1)), integer-programming/MDP-based bug triage incorporating developer calendars ([S-DABT, arXiv:2204.05972](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.05972); [ADPTriage, arXiv:2211.00872](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2211.00872)), and the Google Borg paper as the canonical "OS-style concepts generalize to non-OS, massive-scale workload scheduling" precedent ([Google Research: Borg](https://research.google/pubs/pub43438)). WSJF (Cost of Delay ÷ Job Size), used throughout SAFe/Agile, is effectively "Shortest Job First with a weight term" and is a strong, directly citable precedent for a relative/ordinal priority formula ([Scaled Agile Framework WSJF](https://v5.scaledagileframework.com/?p=3408)) — notably stronger than the Eisenhower Matrix, which despite ubiquitous popular use has no standard numeric scoring formula in any source found, meaning Procura can honestly claim to be "giving the Eisenhower Matrix's intuition an actual algorithmic backbone."

## The AIOps loop and RAG-based root-cause analysis are not hand-waving

Procura's "AI diagnoses and recovers from simulated errors" feature maps with unusual precision onto real, well-documented industry architecture, which means the project can cite specific mechanisms rather than asserting a vague "AI self-healing" claim. The foundational concept is Google's SRE book, which formalizes **toil** as operational work that is "manual, repetitive, automatable, reactive... and devoid of enduring value" and that "grows at least as fast as the growth of its associated service" ([Eliminating Toil, sre.google](https://sre.google/sre-book/eliminating-toil/)) — the entire AIOps industry is the automation of exactly this toil, with Google's own target of keeping toil below 50% of SRE time (actual surveyed average: ~33%). Kubernetes formalizes the generic mechanism underneath this automation as the **control loop**: "a non-terminating loop that regulates the state of a system," illustrated with a thermostat analogy, where controllers "watch the state of your cluster, then make or request changes where needed" to move current state toward desired state ([Kubernetes Controller docs](https://kubernetes.io/docs/concepts/architecture/controller/)). The common "observe → diff → act" framing is a secondary paraphrase of this official language, not a verbatim Kubernetes quote, but it is an accurate one — and it is precisely the loop Procura's error-simulation-and-recovery feature should implement: periodically observe simulated system state, diff it against an expected/healthy state, and act via a bounded, deterministic corrective script.

All four major commercial AIOps platforms converge on the same two-layer architecture Procura proposes. **Datadog Watchdog** runs an always-on, no-manual-configuration anomaly layer plus a distinct "Root Cause Analysis" feature ([Datadog Watchdog docs](https://docs.datadoghq.com/watchdog/)); **Dynatrace Davis AI** performs root cause analysis via "causal AI" using topology, transaction, and code-level information, running an "automatic fault-tree analysis" that folds multiple connected anomalies into one ranked "problem" to reduce alert noise ([Dynatrace Davis AI RCA docs](https://docs.dynatrace.com/docs/platform/davis-ai/root-cause-analysis)); **New Relic's "Proactive Detection"** continuously checks golden signals against normal behavior while "Incident Intelligence" aggregates and correlates alerts ([New Relic AI GA announcement](https://newrelic.com/blog/news/announcing-new-relic-ai-general-availability)). The common pattern — unsupervised/statistical detection with no manual thresholds, then correlation/grouping, then causal/topological root-cause inference, then increasingly LLM-driven remediation — is exactly what Procura compresses into "detect anomaly → diagnose via AI → execute one of N fixed recovery scripts," minus the noise-correlation stage, which is a reasonable simplification at toy scale.

The 2023–2025 academic literature on LLM-based root-cause analysis is the most directly citable validation for Procura's RAG layer, and it is substantial: Microsoft's **RCACopilot** (arXiv:2305.15778, EuroSys '24) is "an on-call system powered by a large language model for automating RCA of cloud incidents"; a follow-up Microsoft paper uses **in-context learning with GPT-4 evaluated over 100,000+ production incidents** specifically to avoid the retraining cost of fine-tuning as new incident data arrives ([arXiv:2401.13810](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.13810)); a ReAct-style agentic approach that retrieves logs, metrics, and database state on demand reports a 7.27% hallucination rate relative to baseline LLM approaches ([arXiv:2403.04123](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.04123)); and **SynergyRCA**, a Kubernetes-specific tool combining RAG over graph databases with GPT-4o, reports average precision of 0.88–0.92 across two production datasets with root causes pinpointed in roughly two minutes ([arXiv:2506.02490](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.02490)). The critical design lesson from this literature is that **flat document retrieval is explicitly criticized as "topology-blind" for infrastructure incidents** — the dominant pattern is RAG over structured sources (graph databases, causal knowledge graphs) rather than naive unstructured-text retrieval. This directly justifies Procura's RAG layer retrieving over structured simulation state (task dependency graphs, resource-allocation tables, process states) rather than flat log text, and it means Procura's existing design choice — reasoning over structured signals rather than open-ended agentic tool use — mirrors the field's own best practice rather than being a simplification to apologize for. On the remediation side, **runbooks** are the real-world term for exactly what Procura calls "recovery scripts": PagerDuty (via its 2020 Rundeck acquisition) and AWS Systems Manager both implement them as named, parameterized, auditable automation documents triggerable automatically or manually ([PagerDuty Runbook Automation](https://www.pagerduty.com/integrations/rundeck-runbook-automation/); [AWS Systems Manager runbooks](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/systems-manager/latest/userguide/application-manager-working-runbooks.html)). AWS explicitly classifies every runbook step by remediation impact — Mutating, Non-mutating, or Undetermined, the last requiring manual review before automatic execution ([AWS remediation impact types](https://docs.aws.eu/systems-manager/latest/userguide/remediation-impact-type.html)) — a strong, specific precedent for Procura's choice to use a small, fixed, pre-vetted set of deterministic recovery actions rather than open-ended LLM-generated remediation commands: this is not a limitation to hide but the documented industry-standard safety pattern.

## Statistical detection beats thresholds, and deadlock handling is messier than textbooks admit

Two of Procura's planned subsystems — anomaly detection and deadlock handling — have a textbook version and a real-world version that diverge sharply, and the research gives Procura exact formulas to close that gap. On anomaly detection, NIST's SEMATECH Handbook provides complete, implementable specifications for all three classical SPC methods. **EWMA** is a recursive smoother, `EWMA_t = λY_t + (1−λ)EWMA_{t-1}`, with control limits `EWMA_0 ± k·s_ewma` where `s_ewma² = [λ/(2−λ)]·s²`; λ is conventionally 0.2–0.3 and k is conventionally 3 ([NIST Handbook §6.3.2.4](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmc/section3/pmc324.htm)). **Z-score** is `Z_i = (Y_i − Ȳ)/s`, but NIST's own page notably does *not* endorse the popular "|Z|>2 or 3" rule of thumb as a formal standard — it instead recommends the more robust **modified Z-score**, `M_i = 0.6745(x_i − x̃)/MAD` (median and median-absolute-deviation based), flagging `|M_i| > 3.5` as a citable, outlier-robust threshold ([NIST Handbook §1.3.5.17](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/eda/section3/eda35h.htm)). This is a genuinely useful correction for Procura to make explicitly: the commonly-used sigma-rule threshold is folk convention, while the modified Z-score is the more defensible choice because mean/SD are themselves distorted by the very outliers being detected. **CUSUM** accumulates signed deviations and flags a shift when `S_hi` or `S_lo` crosses a decision interval h, with both the reference value k and h derivable in closed form from a desired shift size δ, false-alarm rate α, and miss rate β ([NIST Handbook §6.3.2.3](https://www.itl.nist.gov/div898/handbook/pmc/section3/pmc323.htm)) — meaning Procura can expose three business-meaningful knobs (minimum shift to care about, tolerable false-alarm rate, tolerable miss rate) instead of an arbitrary raw threshold, which is the concrete, defensible claim underlying "more rigorous than a naive threshold." Importantly, this is not merely academic: Prometheus ships a native double-exponential-smoothing function for exactly this purpose, Grafana's open-source `promql-anomaly-detection` builds bands from pure recording-rule arithmetic (no ML), and AWS CloudWatch's anomaly detection produces an expected-value band from up to two weeks of trailing data using a documented "statistical and machine learning" hybrid model ([Prometheus query functions docs](https://prometheus.io/docs/prometheus/latest/querying/functions/); [Grafana promql-anomaly-detection](https://github.com/grafana/promql-anomaly-detection); [AWS CloudWatch anomaly detection docs](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/AmazonCloudWatch/latest/monitoring/CloudWatch_Anomaly_Detection.html)) — the "expected-value band" (center ± k·spread) is the same structural pattern across all of them, confirming that SPC-style methods are not just academically grounded but are literally what production monitoring tools use today. The one honest caveat the research surfaced: IT metrics often lack SPC's foundational "stable mean" assumption, so naive SPC still over-fires in bursty systems unless paired with a rolling re-baselining window ([SolarWinds SPC-for-IT blog](https://www.solarwinds.com/blog/4-statistical-process-control-rules-that-detect-anomalies-in-systems)) — Procura should pair its chosen method with a rolling baseline rather than a single fixed historical mean.

Deadlock handling is where the research most sharply overturns a textbook assumption Procura likely starts from. **Banker's Algorithm, while the canonical academic avoidance algorithm, has essentially no production footprint**, and the reason is specific and well-documented: it requires advance, static knowledge of each process's maximum resource claim and a fixed process population, assumptions that "in real systems... it is often not possible" to satisfy, and which also contradict the dynamic process creation/termination of modern systems, while additionally wasting resources reserved against a peak that may never occur ([arXiv 2511.07071](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.07071)). What real systems actually deploy instead spans a clear cost/precision spectrum: optimistic concurrency control and timestamp-ordering (wound-wait) avoid deadlock by construction with no blocking but incur aborts (Google Cloud Spanner explicitly uses wound-wait so "an older transaction never needs to wait for a younger one," at the acknowledged cost of sometimes-unnecessary aborts) ([Life of Spanner Reads & Writes whitepaper](https://docs.cloud.google.com/spanner/docs/whitepapers/life-of-reads-and-writes)); fixed lock ordering is zero-runtime-cost but needs tooling to enforce (the Linux kernel's `lockdep` checker exists precisely because nothing else prevents violating documented lock-order conventions) ([LWN: wait/wound mutexes](https://lwn.net/Articles/548909)); timeout-based abort is cheap but imprecise (MySQL's `innodb_lock_wait_timeout`, offered as an explicit, documented alternative to real detection under high contention) ([MySQL InnoDB Deadlock Detection docs](https://dev.mysql.com/doc/refman/8.0/en/innodb-deadlock-detection.html)); and true wait-for-graph cycle detection is expensive but precise, which is exactly why it is throttled (PostgreSQL only runs its deadlock check after a backend has waited `deadlock_timeout`, default 1 second, "because the check for deadlock is relatively expensive") ([PostgreSQL docs](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/runtime-config-locks.html)) and even disabled under extreme contention in MySQL. Large-scale distributed systems (Spanner, Chubby, ZooKeeper, etcd) mostly skip wait-for-graph detection entirely, substituting lease/TTL-based reclamation — a lock is automatically revoked if its holder fails to renew before expiry — because a crashed or partitioned holder is invisible to graph analysis anyway ([Chubby paper summary](https://blog.acolyer.org/2015/02/13/the-chubby-lock-service-for-loosely-coupled-distributed-systems/)). The classical academic foundation for genuinely distributed detection, Chandy-Misra-Haas's 1983 edge-chasing probe algorithm, is provably correct and complete, but no production distributed database was found actually implementing it — the evidence points to prevention-by-ordering substituting for detection at scale ([Chandy, Misra, Haas 1983](https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/gsez6-b8b74)). This gives Procura a much richer and more honest narrative than "we implement Banker's Algorithm": present Banker's Algorithm as the textbook baseline, then implement timeout-based abort and fixed lock-ordering (with a lockdep-style violation checker) as the actual production-grade upgrade, explicitly framing Banker's as "theoretically important, practically abandoned."

## Benchmarking Procura against real traces makes the comparison honest

The final research thread closes the loop between "Procura simulates scheduling" and "Procura proves its simulation is meaningful." The **Parallel Workloads Archive** (cs.huji.ac.il, maintained by Dror Feitelson) defines the **Standard Workload Format (SWF)**, a flat ASCII, 18-field, one-line-per-job format, and hosts roughly 40 real system logs from 1993–2018 spanning 64-CPU research clusters to Intel NetBatch pools with over 13 million jobs each ([Parallel Workloads Archive — SWF](https://www.cs.huji.ac.il/labs/parallel/workload/swf.html); [Parallel Workloads Archive — Logs](https://www.cs.huji.ac.il/labs/parallel/workload/logs.html)). Because SWF is simple and stable, Procura could write a single parser and replay real historical job arrivals (submit time, requested processors, run time, and more) through its five simulated schedulers — turning "look at this animation" into "here is how FCFS vs. CFS would have actually performed on the CTC SP2 cluster's real 1996 job log." For a cloud/datacenter flavor rather than HPC, **Google's cluster-data traces** (ClusterData2011, one Borg cell over ~1 month; ClusterData2019, eight Borg cells with CPU usage histograms and job-parent relationships) are the standard complementary real-world dataset, released under CC-BY via the `google/cluster-data` GitHub repository ([google/cluster-data](https://github.com/google/cluster-data)) — though ClusterData2019's ~2.4 TiB size means Procura would realistically need a sampled subset rather than the raw trace. Where real traces are unavailable or too unwieldy, the field-standard synthetic alternative is Poisson-arrival generation (exponential inter-arrival times) paired with the **Lublin–Feitelson model**, which uses hyper-Gamma distributions to jointly generate arrival time, requested parallelism, and runtime, calibrated against the Archive's real traces ([Lublin-Feitelson model, cited via IPDPS'06 paper](https://www.cecs.uci.edu/~papers/ipdps06/pdfs/1568974798-IPDPS-paper-1.pdf)) — notably, one comparative study found that the *relative ranking* of scheduling algorithms was largely unaffected by whether real or synthetic workloads were used, which legitimizes starting with synthetic Poisson workloads before layering in real traces.

For metrics, the research gives Procura an unambiguous standard set rather than inventing its own: **makespan** (the completion time of the last job in a batch), **average turnaround time** (completion minus submission, averaged), **average waiting time** (start minus submission), and **utilization**, formally `(Σ size_j × runtime_j) / (M × makespan)` ([Batsim documentation](https://batsim.readthedocs.io/en/main/output-schedule.html); [arXiv 2111.10200](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2111.10200)). **Jain's Fairness Index** has an exact, unambiguous formula and a commonly-misstated range that Procura should get right: `f(x) = (Σxᵢ)² / (n·Σxᵢ²)` ranges from **1/n to 1**, not 0 to 1 — f = 1/n corresponds to total monopolization by one task, and if k of n users get an equal share while the rest get nothing, f evaluates to exactly k/n ([arXiv cs/9809099](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/9809099)). Starvation, by contrast, has no single canonical formula across the literature — papers operationalize it ad hoc, commonly via a fixed wait-time threshold (one GPU-scheduling paper used 30 minutes, finding SJF produced 156 starved jobs versus 12 under a dynamic aging policy) or via maximum observed wait time as a worst-case proxy ([arXiv 2512.10980](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.10980); [arXiv 1503.06301](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1503.06301)) — so Procura should explicitly define its own starvation metric (recommended: max observed wait plus a stated-threshold exceedance rate) rather than claim a nonexistent universal standard. Finally, **CloudSim and SimGrid/Batsim** are the two dominant open-source precedents for building exactly this kind of scheduling simulator — CloudSim for cloud/VM-level resource allocation at scale (its founding paper simulated 100,000 machines), SimGrid/Batsim for HPC-job-level simulation using the same SWF-derived wait/turnaround definitions Procura would adopt ([CloudSim arXiv:0903.2525](https://arxiv.org/pdf/0903.2525); [Batsim docs](https://batsim.readthedocs.io/en/main/output-schedule.html)) — giving Procura direct architectural lineage to cite for "why build a simulator this way" rather than presenting its simulator design as novel in isolation.

## Conclusion

The net effect of this research is that Procura's ambition is not overreaching so much as under-specified relative to what's actually citable: nearly every planned feature has a named, documented, real-world counterpart with an exact formula or architecture, and the gap between "toy OS demo" and "grounded systems project" is mostly a matter of adopting those exact mechanisms rather than inventing approximate ones. The most leveraged single upgrade is swapping the textbook four for CFS/EEVDF as headline algorithms (current production reality, not 1970s theory), paired with Jain's Fairness Index as the quantitative payoff of having built multiple algorithms at all — this alone elevates the project from "simulates scheduling" to "measures and compares scheduling fairness the way production kernel engineers do." The most interesting substantive finding, though, is the asymmetry between the AIOps/RAG pillar and the deadlock pillar: the former is an area where Procura's design (structured-signal RAG, fixed vetted recovery scripts) already mirrors current best practice and 2023-2025 research consensus almost by accident, while the latter requires Procura to actively correct a textbook-driven assumption — Banker's Algorithm should be presented as a historical baseline that the project deliberately supersedes with timeout/lock-ordering, not as the sophisticated endpoint, since no production system found in this research actually runs it. Treated this way, Procura's contribution is less "apply OS theory to to-do lists" (a move with surprisingly little direct academic precedent) and more "give to-do-list prioritization the same rigor — multi-factor weighted priority with decay, fairness measurement, statistically-grounded anomaly detection, and benchmarked evaluation against real workload traces — that production infrastructure schedulers have already proven out."
